# L04 · Resistors set the current — teacher notes

## The idea

The same LED sits behind three different resistors, 220 Ω, 1 kΩ and 4.7 kΩ. A bigger resistance lets less current through, so the LEDs glow bright, medium and dim. This is Ohm's law, current = voltage ÷ resistance, met with a meter rather than a formula.

Learners also meet the colour code. They have to read each resistor to sort them before building. The key on the page is in words, so it works on a black-and-white print.

## Expected readings

With P1 at 4.5 V and red LEDs, red probe on TP1 and black on each TP:

| Resistor | Volts across it | Current (V ÷ kΩ) | LED |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 220 Ω | about 2.6 V | about 12 mA | brightest |
| R2 1 kΩ | about 2.7 V | about 2.7 mA | medium |
| R3 4.7 kΩ | about 2.7 V | about 0.6 mA | dim, but lit |

The surprise worth drawing out: **the voltage across each resistor is almost the same**. Each LED takes about the same 1.8–2 V, and the resistor gets the rest. What changes is the current, and that's what sets brightness.

## Common faults

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Two LEDs equally bright | Resistors swapped; re-read the colours, or measure each one in ohms before placing it |
| Colours hard to read | Red, orange and brown look alike in poor light. Use daylight, or check with the meter's Ω range. |
| LED3 looks dark | It's lit at about 0.6 mA; shade it with a hand |
| A reading of 0 V across a resistor | That branch isn't connected through to GND: check the LED's leads and polarity |
